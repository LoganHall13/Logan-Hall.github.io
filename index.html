Loon Industries

Founder & Mechanical Lead — Loon Industries LLC · Founded 2025 · In Development, Prototype 2 (Loon 1.1)

Borgen Design Competition Finalist — $2,000 Grant, UW–Madison

Loon Industries designs low-cost, 5-axis desktop robotic arms aimed at lowering the barrier to entry into robotics for students and hobbyists. As founder and mechanical lead, I've designed the complete mechanical system for both generations of the arm, while two co-founders lead the software side.

REV A

Loon 1.0

Target: $300 retail / ~$200 build cost. Optimized almost entirely around cost — 3D-printed structure, minimal purchased hardware.

  • 5 axes: 3× NEMA 17 (55 N·cm) + 2× pancake NEMA 17 (23 N·cm)
  • All-belt gear reduction: two 2-stage + two 1-stage gearboxes, plus a planetary/ring gear base
  • Differential mechanism at the wrist — compact 2-axis motion from a single coupled drive
  • Two-sided support structure — zero cantilevered loads, fully enclosed electronics
  • 0.5 lb payload target, ~14" reach
What didn't work: 113 unique parts — belt-gearbox shaft play compounded across stages, hurting rigidity. Shoulder joint complexity made position encoding impossible.
REV B

Loon 1.1

Ground-up redesign. Target raised to $375 / ~$250 cost after 1.0's payload proved insufficient for real use.

  • Purchased planetary gearboxes for rigidity: two 25:1 + one 10:1, plus 3 single-stage belt drives
  • Belt-driven base (replacing the ring gear); all belts now use dedicated tensioners
  • Encoders relocated to motor back (paired with driver), controlled via CAM — solves 1.0's shoulder-encoding gap
  • Sleeker form factor; two motors + gearboxes now exposed as a deliberate weight/aesthetics tradeoff
  • 1 lb payload target (2× improvement), same ~14" reach
Part count: 113 → ~20. Also shifted process: build a rough rigid prototype first, then refine design from what's learned — rather than fully designing before prototyping.

R&D Notes — Cycloidal Gearbox Exploration

While chasing larger reductions with minimal backlash, I explored cycloidal gearboxes as an alternative to belt drives — building 3 printed prototypes and disassembling a purchased commercial unit to study its geometry. Conclusion: a reliable 3D-printed cycloidal gearbox was too costly and mechanically complex to justify at this scale. Purchased planetary gearboxes proved the more cost-effective, rigid solution — a decision that directly shaped Loon 1.1's architecture.

Two seasons of FIRST Robotics Competition (FRC) with Team 2169, King TeC — designing and building a full competitive robot from scratch within a ~2.5 month season.

Sir Morien

Team 2169, King TeC · FRC · Junior Year
Industrial Design Award
[ Add photo of Sir Morien here ]

Challenge

Shoot basketball-sized balls into a hopper 10ft high using AprilTag vision detection, while staying agile enough to maneuver around opposing robots — and physically climb a stepped, inclined pull-up bar structure.

Role & Approach

Mechanical design engineer and technician. Designed a two-way pneumatically actuated flywheel shooter (able to fire at the hopper or redirect opponents' balls), a climbing system using a constant-force spring return and winch with pneumatic dampening, and a two-speed transmission with a dog-shifting clutch. Modeled in Autodesk Inventor with trajectory physics and load calculations sizing the climber and shooter.

Lessons LearnedThe two-way flywheel required slowing/reversing spin to fire the opposite direction, hurting cycle time badly — a single-direction shooter with a hopper feed would've been faster. The two-speed transmission added complexity without a clear payoff; overall drivetrain speed was too cumbersome for the game's pace.

Lady Guinevere

Team 2169, King TeC · FRC "Charged Up" (2023) · Senior Year
Design Innovation — World Championship
[ Add photo of Lady Guinevere here ]

Challenge

2023's "Charged Up" required picking up cones and inflated cubes from the ground, orienting them, and placing cones on poles / cubes on platforms. Most teams relied on a player-handling station that pre-oriented cones upright — the goal here was to skip that station entirely and pick up cones in any orientation directly off the ground.

Role & Approach

Mechanical Captain — designed the entire mechanical system. Built a full swerve-drive drivetrain and a 4-axis arm with a 2-axis wrist, giving enough degrees of freedom to reach and reorient a cone in any ground position. Arm extended over 6ft horizontally; robot stood 8.5ft tall at full vertical extension. Raised/lowered via a winch-driven single-stage extension.

Lessons LearnedThe winch-driven single-stage arm extension was slow relative to the arm's size, making vertical movement cumbersome despite its capability. The claw's reach extended past the front bumper, leaving it exposed to contact from opposing robots — a defensive gap for future iterations.

Result: earned the Design Innovation Award, qualifying for the FIRST World Championship in Houston, TX, competing as a NASA-sponsored team.

A

Polymer Tube Flaring Fixture

Boston Scientific — Internship · Solo Design, Prototype & Fabrication

Designed, prototyped, and fabricated a fixture for flaring the ends of 7mm polymer tubing used in medical device manufacturing. Clamps the tube and lowers it onto a heated conical mandrel to form the flange.

Performed as designed. Main limitation: the cam clamp lacked a positive lock, so it could be pressed open under tight clamping pressure — a self-locking cam geometry would fix this.

Structural Crane Competition

Freshman Year · Small Group (2–3)

Built a crane scored on max weight lifted, min lift time, and min crane weight. Noticed the rules had no size constraint on the crane itself — only on counterweight mass — and used a 6ft beam winched fully horizontal as the counterweight.
